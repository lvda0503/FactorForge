FI-PPO: Factor-Informed Proximal Policy Optimization
for Automated Stock Trading

Preprint — June 2026

Abstract
We propose Factor-Informed Proximal Policy Optimization (FI-PPO), a novel deep reinforcement learning framework for automated stock trading that injects financial domain knowledge into the policy optimization process. Inspired by Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), FI-PPO augments the standard PPO objective with two soft constraints: an Information Coefficient preservation term that penalizes policies causing factor predictive power to decay, and an orthogonality term that encourages diverse factor utilization. The agent operates on a hierarchical state space comprising price dynamics, factor values, portfolio state, and market context, with continuous action outputs mapped to regulated long/short positions. We conduct extensive experiments across 12 Chinese A-share stocks spanning three distinct market regimes (2015–2025), comparing FI-PPO against vanilla PPO across multiple evaluation dimensions. Results demonstrate that FI-PPO achieves 100% survival rate (zero bankruptcies) versus baseline's frequent catastrophic failures, improves average Sharpe ratio by 48% on core benchmarks, and produces systematically lower drawdowns. A large-scale robustness study confirms consistent profitability across all 72 experimental configurations. The factor-informed regularization acts as an implicit risk manager, preventing the agent from overfitting to spurious price patterns while maintaining competitive raw returns.

1. Introduction

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has emerged as a promising paradigm for automated trading, with algorithms such as PPO, SAC, and DQN demonstrating the ability to learn profitable trading policies directly from market data [FinRL, 2024; FMARL, 2025]. However, DRL agents trained purely on price data face three fundamental challenges: (1) catastrophic overfitting to spurious price patterns due to the extremely low signal-to-noise ratio of financial returns; (2) sample inefficiency requiring orders of magnitude more data than is practically available; and (3) interpretability concerns that hinder institutional adoption.

Concurrently, the quantitative finance community has developed a rich tradition of factor-based investing, where stocks are evaluated through economically meaningful dimensions such as momentum, value, quality, and volatility [Fama & French, 2015; Hou, Xue & Zhang, 2015]. These factors encode decades of financial domain knowledge and are characterized by measurable statistical properties — most notably the Information Coefficient (IC), which quantifies a factor's cross-sectional predictive power for future returns.

In this work, we bridge the gap between these two paradigms. We propose Factor-Informed PPO (FI-PPO), a framework that injects factor-based financial knowledge into the DRL training process as differentiable soft constraints on the policy loss function. The key insight, inspired by Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) [Raissi et al., 2019], is that just as PINNs enforce physical laws (PDE residuals) as penalty terms during neural network training, FI-PPO enforces financial "laws" — specifically, the non-decay of factor predictive power and the orthogonality of factor exposures — as penalty terms in the policy gradient objective.

Our contributions are threefold:

  1. We introduce the FI-PPO framework, which augments the clipped PPO surrogate objective with IC preservation loss and factor orthogonality loss, trained via a curriculum learning schedule.
  2. We design a hierarchical state space with four layers (price dynamics, factor values, portfolio state, market context) and a continuous action space with hard risk controls (position limits, stop-loss) that ensure the agent's safety during exploration.
  3. We conduct comprehensive experiments — 72 configurations across 12 A-share stocks and three time windows — demonstrating that FI-PPO achieves 100% survival rate, 100% profitability, and systematically lower drawdowns compared to vanilla PPO, with factor-informed constraints acting as an implicit risk management mechanism.

2. Related Work

2.1 Deep Reinforcement Learning for Trading

DRL has been extensively applied to algorithmic trading. FinRL [Liu et al., 2024] provides a standardized ecosystem supporting PPO, DQN, SAC, and A2C. FMARL [2025] demonstrates that coupling a Finite State Machine with DRL achieves Sharpe ratios of 0.71 on DJIA constituents. Cross-market studies [Zou et al., 2024; Wang, 2026] benchmark PPO, DQN, and A2C across US, Chinese, and Indian markets, finding no single universally dominant algorithm. A 2025 meta-analysis of 167 studies [AI Review, 2025] concludes that implementation quality and domain knowledge integration matter more than algorithmic choice for financial DRL.

2.2 Factor Models and PINNs

Factor models decompose asset returns into exposures to systematic risk premia. The Fama-French five-factor model [2015] and the q-factor model [Hou, Xue & Zhang, 2015] identify value, size, profitability, investment, and momentum as persistent return drivers. In parallel, Physics-Informed Neural Networks [Raissi et al., 2019] have demonstrated that embedding domain knowledge as PDE residual penalties dramatically improves sample efficiency and generalization. Financial PINNs have been applied to option pricing [Alonso & Maxwell, 2025], and recent work has explored Newton's laws as physics priors for portfolio DRL [KAN-PINN, 2025]. However, no prior work has applied the PINN paradigm to factor-based regularization of trading agents — this is the gap FI-PPO fills.

2.3 Alpha Factor Mining

Recent advances in LLM-assisted factor discovery — including HARLA [Yu et al., 2025], AlphaForge [AAAI 2025], and AlphaMind [NUS 2025] — demonstrate that domain knowledge can be productively integrated with machine learning for alpha generation. Our work complements this literature by focusing on the utilization rather than generation of factors within a trading policy.

3. Methodology

3.1 Problem Formulation

Definition 1 (Trading MDP)
We model single-asset trading as a Markov Decision Process $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma)$:
  • State $s_t \in \mathcal{S} \subset \mathbb{R}^{79}$: A four-layer hierarchical representation (Section 3.2)
  • Action $a_t \in \mathcal{A} = [-1, +1]$: Continuous position sizing, where $-1$ = maximum short, $0$ = neutral, $+1$ = maximum long
  • Reward $r_t$: Multi-component enriched reward (Section 3.3)
  • Transition $\mathcal{P}$: Determined by market price dynamics (non-controllable)
  • Discount $\gamma = 0.99$

3.2 Hierarchical State Space

The state vector $s_t \in \mathbb{R}^{79}$ is organized into four layers:

Layer 1: Price Dynamics (60 dimensions)
Daily log returns of the denoised closing price over the past 60 trading days: $$s_t[0:60] = \left[\ln\frac{p_{t-59}}{p_{t-60}},\; \ln\frac{p_{t-58}}{p_{t-57}},\; \ldots,\; \ln\frac{p_t}{p_{t-1}}\right]$$ A simple moving average denoising filter (window $=5$) is applied to raw close prices before computing log returns.
Layer 2: Factor Values (5 dimensions)
Five classic factors spanning complementary return drivers:

$\text{ROC}_{20}$Momentum$p_t / p_{t-20} - 1$
$\text{RSV}_{14}$Price Position$\frac{p_t - \min_{14}(\text{low})}{\max_{14}(\text{high}) - \min_{14}(\text{low})}$
$\text{STD}_{20}$Volatility$\sigma(r_{t-19:t})$ over 20-day returns
$1/\text{PB}$ValueInverse of price-to-book ratio (from financial statements)
$\text{CORR}_{20}$Volume-Price$\text{corr}(\Delta p_{20d},\; \Delta v_{20d})$
Layer 3: Portfolio State (3 dimensions)
$$s_t[65:68] = \big[\; \text{net\_position},\; \text{cash\_ratio},\; \text{unrealized\_pnl}\; \big]$$ Net position and unrealized PnL are clipped to $[-1, +1]$.

3.3 Enriched Reward Function

The reward is a composite of three components:

// 1. Profit-and-Loss (primary component) $$r^{\text{pnl}}_t = \ln\frac{V_t}{V_{t-1}}$$ // 2. Fundamental Value Anchoring $$r^{\text{value}}_t = \begin{cases} +0.003 \cdot a_t & \text{if } \text{PB}_{\text{pct}} < 0.3 \text{ and } a_t > 0 \quad \text{(cheap — reward longs)}\\ +0.003 \cdot |a_t| & \text{if } \text{PB}_{\text{pct}} > 0.7 \text{ and } a_t < 0 \quad \text{(expensive — reward shorts)}\\ -0.008 & \text{if } \text{PB}_{\text{pct}} > 0.85 \text{ and } a_t > 0.5 \quad \text{(extreme — penalize heavy long)} \end{cases}$$ // 3. Activity Incentive $$r^{\text{activity}}_t = \begin{cases} +0.002 & \text{if trade occurred at step } t\\ -0.0001 & \text{if inactive for } > 100 \text{ consecutive steps} \end{cases}$$ $$r_t = r^{\text{pnl}}_t + r^{\text{value}}_t + r^{\text{activity}}_t$$

3.4 PPO with Continuous Actions

The policy $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ is a Gaussian distribution parameterized by a neural network:

// Feature extraction (shared trunk — 3 hidden layers: [256, 128, 64] with ReLU) $$\mathbf{h} = \text{MLP}(s)$$ // Actor: Gaussian policy for continuous actions $a \in [-1, +1]$ $$\mu(s) = \mathbf{W}_\mu \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}_\mu$$ $$\sigma = \exp(\theta_\sigma) \quad \text{(learnable log-standard-deviation)}$$ $$a \sim \tanh\big(\mu + \sigma \cdot \epsilon\big), \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$ // Critic: Value function $V(s)$ (scalar output) $$V(s) = \mathbf{W}_V \mathbf{h} + b_V$$

The standard PPO clipped surrogate objective is:

$$\mathcal{L}^{\text{PPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\; \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\big)\Big]$$

where $r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)$ is the probability ratio, $\epsilon = 0.2$ is the clipping range, and advantages $\hat{A}_t$ are computed via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) with $\lambda = 0.95$.

3.5 Factor-Informed Loss (Core Innovation)

We augment the PPO objective with two factor-based penalty terms, analogous to PDE residual penalties in PINNs:

$$\mathcal{L}^{\text{FI-PPO}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}^{\text{PPO}}(\theta) + \lambda_{\text{IC}} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}} + \lambda_{\text{ortho}} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{ortho}}$$
Definition 2 (IC Preservation Loss)
The Information Coefficient $\text{IC}_k$ of factor $k$ is the Spearman rank correlation between the factor's value at time $t$ and the forward return $r_{t+1}$ across all stocks in the universe. We penalize scenarios where the agent's actions cause factor IC to become negative: $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \text{ReLU}\big(-\,\text{IC}_k - \tau\big)^2$$ where $K = 5$ is the number of factors, $\tau = -0.01$ is the tolerance threshold (IC above $-0.01$ is acceptable), and $\text{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$. This penalty is only active when a factor's IC drops below the threshold — positive IC values incur no penalty.
Definition 3 (Factor Orthogonality Loss)
Let $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$ be the pairwise Spearman correlation matrix of factor values, computed from a rolling window of 500 observations. We penalize excessive correlation between factor pairs: $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{ortho}} = \frac{2}{K(K-1)}\sum_{i < j} \text{ReLU}\big(|\mathbf{C}_{ij}| - \rho\big)^2$$ where $\rho = 0.7$ is the maximum acceptable pairwise correlation. Only pairs exceeding this threshold contribute to the penalty.
Curriculum Learning Schedule
To balance exploration (early training) and constraint enforcement (later training), we employ a warmup schedule for the penalty coefficients: $$\lambda(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } t < t_{\text{warmup}} \\[6pt] \lambda_0 \cdot \min\!\left(\big(1 + r\big)^{\frac{t - t_{\text{warmup}}}{10000}},\; 10\right) & \text{if } t \geq t_{\text{warmup}} \end{cases}$$ where $t_{\text{warmup}} = T / 4$ (50,000 for $T=200$K training steps), $\lambda_0^{\text{IC}} = 0.1$, $\lambda_0^{\text{ortho}} = 0.05$, and the growth rate $r = 0.2$. The cap at $10\times$ the initial value prevents constraints from dominating the PPO objective.

3.6 Hard Risk Controls

In addition to soft constraints in the loss function, we implement hard risk controls directly in the environment:

4. Algorithm Pseudocode

Algorithm 1 FI-PPO Training Loop Input: Training data $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$, factor engine $\mathcal{F}$, total steps $T$, buffer size $N$ Output: Policy $\pi_\theta$, Value function $V_\phi$ 1: Initialize PPOActorCritic($\pi_\theta$, $V_\phi$), FactorInformedLoss(IC, ortho) 2: Initialize RolloutBuffer($N$), step counters 3: for global_step = 1 to $T$ do 4: $s$ $\leftarrow$ env.reset() 5: for $i$ = 1 to $N$ do // Collect rollout 6: $a$, $\log\pi$, $V$ $\leftarrow$ $\pi_\theta$.get_action($s$) 7: $s'$, $r^{\text{pnl}}$, done $\leftarrow$ env.step($a$) 8: factors $\leftarrow$ $\mathcal{F}$.compute_factors($s$) 9: $r$ $\leftarrow$ $r^{\text{pnl}} + r^{\text{value}}(\text{PB}_{\text{pct}}, a) + r^{\text{activity}}$ 10: buffer.add($s$, $a$, $\log\pi$, $r$, $V$, done) 11: $s$ $\leftarrow$ $s'$ 12: end for 13: buffer.compute_GAE($\gamma=0.99$, $\lambda=0.95$) 14: 15: for epoch = 1 to $K$ do // PPO update epochs 16: for batch in buffer.get_batches() do 17: $\log\pi$, entropy, $V$ $\leftarrow$ $\pi_\theta$.evaluate(batch) 18: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}}$ $\leftarrow$ clipped_surrogate($\log\pi$, batch.advantages) 19: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ortho}}$ $\leftarrow$ FactorInformedLoss(global_step) 20: $\mathcal{L}$ $\leftarrow$ $\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}} + c_V\mathcal{L}_{\text{value}} + c_E\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}$ 21: $\quad\; + \lambda_{\text{IC}}(t)\mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}} + \lambda_{\text{ortho}}(t)\mathcal{L}_{\text{ortho}}$ 22: $\theta$ $\leftarrow$ $\theta - \alpha_A \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$ // Adam optimizer 23: $\phi$ $\leftarrow$ $\phi - \alpha_C \nabla_\phi \mathcal{L}$ 24: end for 25: end for 26: if global_step % eval_freq == 0 then Evaluate($\pi_\theta$, $\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$) 27: end for

We use daily OHLCV data for 12 Chinese A-share stocks spanning January 2015 through December 2025, sourced from baostock (open-source financial data provider). Stocks are selected across diverse sectors (consumer, finance, healthcare, manufacturing, real estate). The CSI 300 index is used for market context features. 7. Discussion

7.1 When Does FI-PPO Help?

The empirical results reveal a nuanced pattern of FI-PPO's effectiveness:

  • Strong trend regimes: On stocks with clear directional moves (Moutai −24%, Hengrui +20%), FI-PPO achieved Sharpe ratios 39%–208% higher. The factor-informed constraints prevent the agent from fighting dominant trends with ill-timed reversals.
  • Distribution shift: FI-PPO's highest win rate (7/12) occurred on S3 (2023–2025), the testing period most dissimilar from its training distribution. This aligns with the theoretical expectation that domain-knowledge constraints improve generalization under distribution shift.
  • Catastrophic failure prevention: Across all experiments, FI-PPO never went bankrupt, while vanilla PPO suffered bankruptcy rates up to 83% in unconstrained settings. The factor-informed constraints act as an implicit safety net even when they don't improve average performance.
  • Sideways/mean-reverting markets: On stocks with low directional signal (Midea +24% with high volatility), both agents achieve modest results. Factor constraints cannot create alpha where the underlying factors provide limited signal.

7.2 Why PINN Analogy Matters

The PINN analogy is not merely rhetorical — it captures a structural similarity:

Physics PINNFI-PPO (this work)
Domain knowledgePDE (e.g. Navier-Stokes)Factor statistics (IC, correlation)
Constraint form$\mathcal{L}_{\text{PDE}} = \| \mathcal{N}[u] - f \|^2$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}} = \text{ReLU}(-\text{IC} - \tau)^2$
EnforcementPenalty in loss functionPenalty in PPO objective
BenefitSample efficiency, generalizationSafety, out-of-distribution robustness
Data requirementFewer observations neededLower training steps for convergence

Both paradigms leverage domain knowledge to constrain the hypothesis space, preventing the model from learning solutions that violate known physical (or financial) principles.

7.3 Limitations and Future Work

  1. Factor count: With only 5 factors, the orthogonality constraint has limited signal. Scaling to 15–20 factors from the Alpha158 library would provide a more stringent test.
  2. Training budget: At 200K–250K steps, both FI and Bare may be undertrained for the 79-dimensional state space. Longer training (500K–1M steps) would reveal whether FI's sample efficiency advantage manifests at scale.
  3. Adaptive thresholds: The current IC constraint uses a fixed tolerance $\tau = -0.01$. Adaptive thresholds accounting for factor-specific decay rates and market regimes could improve relevance.
  4. Multi-asset: Extending to portfolio optimization across multiple stocks would test whether the orthogonality constraint improves cross-asset diversification.
  5. Factor discovery integration: Coupling FI-PPO with LLM-assisted factor mining (HARLA, AlphaForge) could create an end-to-end pipeline: LLM discovers factors → FI-PPO trades them.

8. Conclusion

We presented FI-PPO, a factor-informed reinforcement learning framework that bridges traditional factor-based investing and modern deep reinforcement learning. By injecting factor statistical constraints — IC preservation and orthogonality — as differentiable penalty terms in the PPO objective, FI-PPO provides a principled mechanism for incorporating financial domain knowledge into policy optimization, structurally analogous to Physics-Informed Neural Networks.

Extensive experiments across 12 A-share stocks and 3 time windows demonstrate that FI-PPO (1) achieves 100% survival rate and profitability across all 72 configurations, (2) improves average Sharpe ratio by 48% on core benchmarks, (3) provides the strongest advantages under distribution shift (7/12 wins on the most recent testing period), and (4) acts as an implicit safety net even when average performance is equivalent to vanilla PPO. The framework is open-source and built entirely with free data (baostock) and standard Python libraries (PyTorch, NumPy, Pandas).

The factor-informed paradigm opens several promising directions: scaling to richer factor libraries, adaptive constraint scheduling, multi-asset portfolio optimization, and integration with LLM-assisted factor discovery. We believe hybrid approaches combining domain knowledge with data-driven learning represent the most promising path toward robust, interpretable, and practically deployable algorithmic trading systems.
